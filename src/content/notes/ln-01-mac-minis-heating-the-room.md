Confession time. I work at Splunk. I get to spend my days with some of the best and brightest Splunkers on the planet. And until recently, the Splunk environment in my own house was... let's call it decorative.

It wasn't for lack of toys. I've got a little stack of Mac minis running Proxmox, a Home Assistant box that runs most of the house, a NAS, cameras, and more sensors than any family needs. Buying the stuff was never the problem. The follow-through was. Getting all of it talking to each other and doing something useful.

![14 of the 45 commits to the home-lab repo from Oct 27 to 30](/notes/ln-01/commits.png)

In my defense, life happened, all at once. We moved back to the US from Australia. We started a family. Work got busy. Any one of those is a good reason to leave the lab alone for a while. All three together, and the lab did what home labs do when nobody's watching. It kept running, mostly as a space heater.

If you have a lab of your own, you know the rest of the list. The project that needs one more cable you don't own. The update you'll get to this weekend, every weekend. The new toy that arrives before the last one works. Kids who need you a lot more than your VLANs do. None of it is a disaster on its own. Stack it up, and that's how a lab ends up decorative.

As a Splunk employee, that stung a bit. So I decided to do something about it instead of letting a perfectly good lab sit there burning electricity.

## The ground rules

First, the WAF. If you run Home Assistant, you already know the most important metric in the house is the Wife Approval Factor. The rule: don't break the lights, the locks or bedtime. I've spent years avoiding light switches and I'm not going back now.

Second, I'm using AI for a good chunk of this, and I'll say so. It types faster than me and never gets tired of my questions. It doesn't replace knowing how the pieces should fit together, though. Somebody still has to be the architect, and somebody has to notice when the answer is confidently wrong. I'll show you when that happens.

Third, write it all down. What I built, what was hard, what turned out to be worth it, and what I broke along the way.

## What's coming

A short Lab Note every Tuesday, plus a quick bite on LinkedIn when I find a good one. Each Lab Note is a single thing from the lab: what I tried, what surprised me, how you could try it in your own lab, and why the same lesson shows up in the enterprise. Security, observability, data pipelines, AI. The stuff I talk about all day, just with smaller stakes and more blinking lights.

## Why write it down at all

I think most of us carry a little imposter syndrome. We're trying to be the smartest person in the room while hoping nobody notices we're figuring it out as we go. I'm no different. So consider this me showing my work, half-finished projects and all.

And if it nudges you to put down the doomscroll and pick up a new toy, or dust off the one sitting in your drawer, even better. If you're not having fun with technology, why do you work in it?
